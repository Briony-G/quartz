Ivy Weathervane

##### General details about Ivy
Ivy is a woman of 6 foot when standing tall and proud, though she often likes to play the stooped weak old woman in an attempt to appear unthreatening at 5’ 5”. She has straggly grey hair, and full length robes in natural browns and greens, practical for her work outdoors in the forest, and for her penchant for appearing from thin air in front of unsuspecting townsfolk straying too near her cottage.

**Age** - 102
**Likes** - whisky, people’s respect and fear, a quiet evening in an armchair by the fire with her daughter, gossip
**Dislikes** - disrespect, overly extravagant attire, people knowing too much about her




### Backstory
##### Summary
Ivy is stubborn and proud, shaped by decades of discipline in the convent and honed by years of solitary life as a forest herbalist. She is aloof, slow to trust, and often guarded in her words, a reflection of the cold distance she once learned to navigate under [[Mother Abbess Phellon]]. Yet beneath that reserve lies a deep, practical knowledge of herbs and remedies, cultivated through secret study, patient observation, and a lifetime of tending both garden and illness. Her years raising [[Rosemary]] have softened some of her edges, teaching her a measured warmth and quiet care, but she remains a woman who values independence, discipline, and skill above sentiment (though she doesn’t always apply that to herself!) as someone who both commands respect and inspires caution in equal measure.

##### Backstory TLDR
**Early life:** Foundling left at the [[Convent of Still Dawn]] with a small herbalist’s token (rare mountain thyme); raised under strict, distant [[Mother Abbess Phellon]].

**Childhood in convent:** Obedient, intelligent, drawn to the overgrown garden; conflicted feelings toward [[Mother Abbess Phellon|Phellon]]—respect for her authority, dislike of control.

**Mentorship:** Befriended [[Sister Elowen]], the librarian, who guided her studies in herbs, foraging, and remedies.

**Secret practice:** Began quietly cultivating and distributing medicinal herbs to townsfolk while still at the convent.

**Departure from convent:** Left after [[Sister Elowen]] died from an illness Ivy could not cure; hardened emotionally, became solitary.

**Life as herbalist:** Settled in the forest; gained reputation as skilled healer; respected, feared, and occasionally whispered about by townsfolk.

**Rosemary:** Took in a foundling named [[Rosemary]] in later years; raised her with kindness tempered by strictness; taught her herbalism and forest skills.

**Catalyst for adventure:** [[Rosemary]] was mysteriously abducted from Ivy’s home; this drives Ivy to leave her forest home and embark on her quest.

##### Early Life
Ivy arrived at the convent as a foundling, left on its steps before dawn with no name and no note, only a small token tied to her swaddling—a dried sprig of rare mountain thyme dried within a small glass vial, its scent still faintly alive. The sisters took it as a practical thing, perhaps a sign of an herbalist’s hand, and recorded it carefully in their ledgers in case her mother ever returned to claim her. Ivy kept the charm all her life, worn smooth by time, a quiet promise that she had been loved enough to be remembered, even if only in leaves and glass.

Ivy’s earliest memories were not of family, but of stone corridors and the constant hush of the Convent of Still Dawn. It was a place where routine shaped every hour and silence was treated as a form of reverence. The sisters believed discipline brought clarity, and children were raised to fit seamlessly into the order’s expectations. Questions were permitted, but only the right ones; individuality was not punished, merely overlooked.

At the head of the convent stood Mother Abbess Phellon. She was a composed woman, immaculate in posture and word, whose calm never seemed to waver. Phellon was not unkind, but she was distant in a way that felt immovable, as if her thoughts were always fixed on something higher and far away. Praise from her was rare, not because Ivy failed, but because Phellon believed approval was unnecessary. Duty, she taught, should be its own reward.

Ivy learned to read Phellon’s silences as carefully as others read scripture. A nod meant acceptance. A pause meant disappointment. There was no anger in the abbess’s corrections, only certainty—an unwavering belief that the convent’s way was the correct one. Phellon spoke often of order, of pruning excess so that the soul might grow straight and true. Ivy listened, absorbed, and obeyed, even when the lessons left her feeling oddly unseen.

She excelled at her studies and her chores, but it was in the convent’s small, neglected garden that Ivy felt most herself, perhaps as it gave her an imagined connection to her mother. The abbess rarely visited it, deeming it unimportant, and so it remained a quiet refuge. Ivy tended the stubborn plants there, finding comfort in their persistence. Growth did not need permission; it only needed care. Though she never would have framed it this way as a child, Ivy sensed a truth the convent never named—that life could be guided without being controlled.

Mother Phellon noticed Ivy’s attentiveness to the garden and cautioned her gently, reminding her that focus was a limited resource and should be devoted to higher callings. Ivy accepted the guidance with a bowed head, but an inner fury. She vowed that when she had found her own freedom she would shape her own interests and destiny, and never truly bow to anyone again. The convent taught Ivy patience, restraint, and endurance—but it was within those quiet limits that she first learned to nurture something of her own, unseen and quietly resilient.

Ivy’s feelings toward Mother Abbess Phellon were never simple. She respected the abbess’s bearing, the quiet authority she carried, and the unquestioned respect she commanded from everyone around her. Yet beneath that admiration lay a persistent unease—an aversion to how completely Phellon’s certainty shaped the lives beneath her, Ivy’s included. She learned to honour the woman without loving the power she held, and to admire strength while quietly yearning to stand beyond its reach.

##### Life as a Herbalist
As Ivy grew into adulthood, she learned how to exist in the narrow spaces the convent allowed—and how to make use of the ones it overlooked. Her work in the garden expanded from quiet tending into careful cultivation, and soon she was growing herbs the infirm sisters found eased pain, settled fevers, or brought restful sleep. Word spread softly, never announced, passed in murmurs between lay visitors, travellers seeking shelter, and townsfolk who knew which sister to ask for “the one who knows plants.” Ivy never charged coin openly; favours, ingredients, and quiet gratitude were safer currencies, and far less likely to draw notice.

Her closest ally in this quiet life was the elderly convent librarian, Sister Elowen, a woman half-forgotten by most and content to be so. Elowen noticed Ivy’s questions and, without comment, began setting aside books—old herbal indexes, travellers’ journals, marginal notes penned by long-dead hands. Under Elowen’s guidance, Ivy learned to read not just words but patterns: where certain plants thrived, how soil and season altered potency, which remedies healed and which merely numbed. The garden became her classroom, the library her map of the wider world. Together, without ever naming it rebellion, they built something living and useful within the convent’s walls—knowledge passed hand to hand, root to root, quietly growing beyond anyone’s control.

Sister Elowen’s death came quietly, and all the more cruelly for it. An illness Ivy recognised but could not unravel took hold, resisting every poultice, tea, and whispered hope she knew. Elowen faced it with the same calm she had given her books away, but Ivy was left with a sharp, enduring sense of failure. When the convent bells rang for the last time in Elowen’s honour, Ivy did not stay long after. She left without ceremony, carrying what knowledge she had gathered and little else, and built a life alone at the edge of the forest. There, she worked as a herbalist for any who came to her door, but something in her had hardened. Kindness became measured, trust earned slowly, and hope was treated like a fragile thing—never offered lightly again.

Over time, the people of the surrounding towns learned her name, and then learned to speak it carefully. Ivy’s remedies worked where others failed, her advice was blunt and unsentimental, and her prices were never coin alone. Some said she demanded truths instead—confessions, promises kept, small acts of change. Her reputation was amplified by the oddness of her presence: robes patched with pockets bulging with strange herbs, roots, and dried plants, carrying scents both sweet and pungent, sometimes sharp enough to make a visitor cough or wrinkle their nose. Children were warned not to wander too deep into the woods lest “Old Ivy” see them, while the sick were quietly sent down forest paths with desperate faith. Stories grew in the telling: that she spoke to the plants, that the forest bent to her will, that she could just as easily refuse aid as grant it. Helpful, certainly—but never gentle, and never entirely safe. In time, Ivy became less a woman and more a presence, a figure woven into rumour and necessity alike, respected, feared, and remembered long before anyone knew how old she truly was. It was known that when asking for things beyond medical advice, she would skirt around the answer, or give conflicting accounts, until the real truth of her life was a mystery.

##### Relationship with Rosemary
In her later years, when Ivy had begun to think of her life as something already written, the past found its way back to her doorstep. One morning, just before dawn, she discovered a small bundled infant resting where the forest path met her door, left with the same careful deliberation Ivy herself had once known. There was no note—only a sprig of fresh rosemary tucked into the blanket, its sharp, clean scent cutting through the morning air. Ivy stood there for a long time before lifting the child, feeling the quiet weight of choice settle into her hands.

She named the girl [[Rosemary]] and raised her with a firmness born of experience rather than doctrine. Rosemary’s childhood was kind but unyielding: lessons were expected to be learned, chores completed, and excuses held to little value. Ivy taught her how to read weather in leaves, how to harvest without killing the root, how to listen when someone spoke of pain rather than rushing to cure it. Rosemary grew up at Ivy’s side, assisting with remedies, gathering plants, and greeting townsfolk who arrived with equal parts hope and hesitation. Where Ivy had once been shaped by cold stone walls, Rosemary was shaped by soil, seasons, and a woman who loved her fiercely but quietly. Love was not a thing spoken, but something shown in a proud nod, or a gentle tucking of the blanket around her while she slept. In caring for the child, Ivy found a softness she had not allowed herself in many years—not enough to undo the hardness life had carved into her, but enough to dull its sharpest edges and let warmth return in small, carefully guarded ways.
##### Ivy's Call to Adventure
The night Rosemary was taken shattered the careful stillness Ivy had built her life upon. Ivy woke to find the girl’s bed unmade, blankets half-thrown aside as if sleep had been torn from her, and the wardrobe untouched—clothes, boots, and the small pouch Rosemary never went anywhere without all still in their places. The door stood open to the forest air, but there were no tracks Ivy could trust, no signs of struggle she could name with certainty, and no magic she could easily follow—only absence, heavy and deliberate. It was clear Rosemary had not left by choice. For the first time in many years, Ivy abandoned her home without knowing when—or if—she would return. The mystery of Rosemary’s disappearance became the fire that drove her onward, turning a woman long content to endure into one who would cross any road, bargain with any power, and face whatever waited in the dark to bring her child home. She broke the lifetime’s worth of routine and habit, and left the forest she had spent her whole life, unable to imagine quite how different the wider world would appear, or quite how little she seemed to matter outside her own small sphere of influence.
